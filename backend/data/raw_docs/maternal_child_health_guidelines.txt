GUIDELINES FOR ANTENATAL CARE, SKILLED ATTENDANCE AT BIRTH, AND NEWBORN CARE
Maternal Health Division, Department of Family Welfare
Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, Government of India

Note: This document was originally written for ANMs (Auxiliary Nurse Midwives) and health workers, but contains extensive guidance directly relevant to expectant mothers and families. The content below focuses on the publicly-relevant portions: what to expect, danger signs, nutrition, and newborn care.

ANTENATAL CARE (CARE DURING PREGNANCY)

Key Messages:
- Every pregnancy is at risk - even with the best screening, one cannot predict which woman will develop complications.
- Good record-keeping and regular checkups assist in early detection of problems.
- Ensure iron-folic acid (IFA) supplementation for every pregnant woman.
- Do not take any medication during the first trimester unless specifically advised by a doctor.
- Adequate rest and diet during pregnancy result in better outcomes for both mother and baby.

Registration and Number of Visits:
- The first antenatal visit should ideally take place in the first trimester (before 12 weeks of pregnancy), as soon as pregnancy is suspected.
- At least 4 ANC visits are recommended: 1st visit at registration, 2nd visit between 4th-6th month (around 26 weeks), 3rd visit in the 8th month (around 32 weeks), and 4th visit in the 9th month (around 36 weeks).
- An antenatal card should be completed and given to the woman; she should carry it to every visit and to delivery.

Weight Gain:
- A pregnant woman should normally gain 9-11 kg during her pregnancy - approximately 2 kg per month (0.5 kg per week) after the first trimester.
- Inadequate weight gain (less than 2 kg/month) may indicate poor nutrition and can lead to a low birth-weight baby - food supplementation may be needed.
- Excessive weight gain (more than 3 kg/month) should prompt a checkup for pre-eclampsia or multiple pregnancy.

Symptoms That Indicate Discomfort (usually normal): nausea and vomiting, heartburn, constipation, increased urination frequency.

Symptoms That May Indicate a Complication (seek care promptly): fever, vaginal discharge, palpitations/easy fatigue/breathlessness at rest, generalized swelling of the body or facial puffiness, passing smaller amounts of urine, vaginal bleeding, decreased or absent fetal movements, leaking of watery fluid.

Laboratory Investigations Recommended During Pregnancy:
- Haemoglobin (Hb) testing: at first visit and again at 28 weeks. Hb below 11 g/dl is considered anaemia (7-11 g/dl = moderate, below 7 g/dl = severe anaemia requiring urgent referral).
- Blood group testing (important in case blood transfusion is needed).
- Urine testing for protein (screens for pre-eclampsia) and sugar (screens for gestational diabetes).

Iron-Folic Acid (IFA) Supplementation:
- All pregnant women should take one IFA tablet daily (100 mg elemental iron + 0.5 mg folic acid) for at least 100 days, starting after the first trimester (14-16 weeks).
- Women with anaemia (Hb below 11 g/dl) need two IFA tablets per day for three months (at least 200 tablets total).
- Common side effects (black stools, mild nausea) are normal and not dangerous - do not stop taking the tablets. Taking tablets with meals (rather than on an empty stomach) can reduce nausea. Avoid taking with tea or coffee, as this reduces iron absorption.

Tetanus Toxoid (TT) Vaccination:
- Two doses of TT are given during pregnancy to prevent neonatal tetanus. The first dose is given after the first trimester; the second dose one month later (but at least one month before the expected delivery date).

Diet During Pregnancy:
- A pregnant woman needs about 300 extra kcal per day compared to her usual diet.
- Diet should be rich in proteins, iron, vitamin A, and other micronutrients: cereals, milk and milk products, green leafy vegetables, pulses, eggs and meat/fish (if non-vegetarian), nuts (especially groundnuts), jaggery, fruits.
- Foods rich in vitamin C (lemon, amla, guava, oranges) help iron absorption when eaten alongside iron-rich foods.
- Avoid tobacco and alcohol completely during pregnancy.
- No medication should be taken unless prescribed by a qualified health practitioner.
- Rest: 8 hours of sleep at night plus 2 hours of rest during the day is recommended; avoid heavy lifting and strenuous work.

Danger Signs During Pregnancy - Seek Emergency Care Immediately (day or night, without waiting) if experiencing:
- Any vaginal bleeding during pregnancy, or heavy bleeding (soaking more than 2-3 pads in 20-30 minutes) during or after delivery
- Severe headache with blurred vision
- Convulsions or loss of consciousness
- Labour lasting longer than 12 hours
- Placenta not delivered within 30 minutes of the baby's birth
- Labour starting before 8 months (preterm labour)
- Water breaking before labour starts
- Continuous severe abdominal pain

Seek care at a health centre promptly if experiencing:
- High fever with or without abdominal pain, feeling too weak to get out of bed
- Fast or difficult breathing
- Decreased or absent fetal movements
- Excessive vomiting with inability to keep anything down

Birth Preparedness:
- All pregnant women are strongly encouraged to opt for an institutional delivery (at a hospital/health facility), since complications can develop suddenly and are not always predictable, and facilities have staff, equipment, and referral systems available.
- Families should identify a skilled birth attendant, arrange transportation in advance, and know the location of the nearest functioning health facility.
- Having blood donors identified in advance (family/community members) can be life-saving in case of haemorrhage.

Sex During Pregnancy: Generally safe throughout a normal pregnancy. Should be avoided if there is a history of recurrent miscarriage or preterm labour.

POSTPARTUM CARE (AFTER DELIVERY)

More than 50% of maternal deaths occur during the postpartum period, and the first 48 hours after delivery are the most critical.

Two postnatal check-up visits are recommended: the first within 48 hours of delivery, and the second within 7-10 days.

Danger Signs After Delivery - Seek Emergency Care Immediately:
- Excessive vaginal bleeding (soaking more than 2-3 pads in 20-30 minutes), or bleeding that increases rather than decreases after delivery
- Convulsions
- Fast or difficult breathing
- Fever and weakness such that the woman cannot get out of bed
- Severe abdominal pain

Seek care promptly (non-emergency but should not be delayed) for:
- Fever
- Abdominal pain or feeling generally unwell
- Swollen, red, or tender breasts, or sore/cracked nipples
- Painful or difficult urination
- Pain in the perineum or pus discharge from the area
- Foul-smelling vaginal discharge (may indicate infection)

Postpartum feelings of sadness or crying easily, especially after the first week, may indicate postpartum depression and should be discussed with a health provider.

Postpartum Nutrition: A breastfeeding mother needs approximately 550 extra kcal/day for the first six months, and 400 extra kcal/day for the next six months, compared to her pre-pregnancy diet.

Family Planning After Delivery: A woman can become pregnant as early as six weeks after delivery if she has sex and is not exclusively breastfeeding. Couples should plan for birth spacing - a gap of 3-5 years between pregnancies is healthier for both mother and child.

BREASTFEEDING AND INFANT FEEDING

- Breastfeeding should be initiated within half an hour of a normal delivery (or within two hours of a caesarean section).
- Colostrum (the first milk, often yellowish) is highly nutritious and rich in protective antibodies - it should NOT be discarded, and pre-lacteal feeds (giving anything else before breastfeeding) should be avoided.
- Exclusive breastfeeding is recommended for the first 6 months - only breast milk, not even water, is needed during this period.
- After 6 months, the baby needs complementary (solid/semi-solid) food in addition to continued breastfeeding, as breast milk alone is no longer sufficient.
- Demand feeding (feeding whenever the baby shows hunger cues) is recommended over feeding by a fixed schedule.
- Night feeding helps babies sleep more soundly and should be encouraged, not discouraged.
- Feeding bottles should be avoided due to hygiene and infection risks.

ESSENTIAL NEWBORN CARE

Key Messages:
- Keep the newborn warm - babies can die of hypothermia (low body temperature).
- Ensure a clean cord cut, clean cord tie, and clean, dry cord stump to prevent infection.
- Nothing should be applied to the umbilical cord stump - it should be kept clean and dry, and will fall off on its own.

Keeping the Baby Warm:
- Dry the baby immediately after birth with a clean, warm towel, then wrap in fresh dry cloth.
- Skin-to-skin contact with the mother helps maintain the baby's body temperature.
- Avoid bathing the newborn on the first day - the first bath should be postponed and done in a warm room with warm water.
- Normal newborn body temperature is between 36.5°C and 37°C. Hypothermia (below 36°C) is dangerous and requires immediate warming.

Danger Signs in a Newborn - Seek Care Immediately:
- Poor breastfeeding or refusing to feed
- The baby looks unwell/ill
- Fever or the baby feels cold to touch
- Fast or difficult breathing
- Blood in the stool

Immunization: The family should be advised on the national immunization schedule for the newborn, as recommended by their health provider.
